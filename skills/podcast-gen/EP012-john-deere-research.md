# EP012 Research: John Deere Right-to-Repair Settlement

*Research compiled: April 9, 2026 | Sources: AP News, Reuters, The Register, AgWeb, The Drive, KCRG, Farm Policy News, Farm Action, Carscoops, Regulatory Oversight, The Verge, Des Moines Register*

---

## The Core Thesis

John Deere's $99 million settlement is the largest right-to-repair payout in history — but on a company that makes $5 billion in annual profit by locking farmers out of their own machines, it's closer to a speeding ticket than a reckoning. The headline is real progress; the fine print is why the fight isn't over. Farmers won a battle, John Deere kept its war chest, and the FTC is still circling.

---

## What Actually Happened

### The Settlement (April 6–7, 2026)
- Deere & Company agreed to pay **$99 million** into a settlement fund, resolving a **2022 class action lawsuit** filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois.
- Fund recipients: any farmer or farm that **paid Deere or its authorized dealers for large agricultural equipment repairs between January 10, 2018 and the settlement's preliminary approval date**.
- Equipment covered: **large agricultural equipment** — tractors, combines, and sugarcane harvesters specifically named.
- Recovery rate for plaintiffs: **26–53% of overcharge damages** — significantly above the typical class action recovery of 5–15%.
- **No admission of wrongdoing.** Deere's statement: "This settlement addresses the issues raised in the 2022 complaint and brings this case to an end with no finding of wrongdoing."
- Settlement still requires **judge's final approval**.

### The Injunctive Relief (the non-cash part)
- Deere agreed to make available, for **10 years**, "the digital tools required for the maintenance, diagnosis, and repair" of large ag equipment.
- By **December 31, 2026**: farmers must be able to perform reprogramming and diagnostic functions through John Deere Operations Center PRO Service in **offline mode**, AND access the same repair tools as the company's **Dealer Technical Assistance Center** (advanced remote repair tooling).
- Access to future repair resources is secured once tools are available to over 50% of official Deere dealers.
- Deere's VP of Aftermarket & Customer Support, **Denver Caldwell**: "We're pleased that this resolution allows us to move forward and remain focused on what matters most — serving our customers."

### The Separate FTC Lawsuit — Still Active
- **January 2025** (Biden's last days): the FTC, plus the states of **Minnesota and Illinois**, filed a separate antitrust lawsuit against Deere. Michigan, Wisconsin, and Arizona have since joined.
- The FTC accuses Deere of creating a **repair services monopoly** via its "Service ADVISOR" diagnostic tool — the version available to farmers and independent shops is allegedly crippled compared to the full dealer version.
- A U.S. judge ruled in mid-2025 that Deere must face this lawsuit — the case is currently in **discovery**.
- Critical wrinkle: the new FTC chairman, **Andrew Ferguson** (Trump appointee), **dissented** from the original vote to file the complaint, calling it "premature." He can't withdraw it without a majority vote. But if Republican commissioner **Mark Meador** is confirmed, that math changes. The states have said they'll proceed independently regardless.
- This lawsuit seeks: declaration that Deere violated antitrust laws, permanent injunction, full version of Service ADVISOR made public, civil penalties.

## Timeline: The Six-Year Fight

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| ~2015–2017 | DMCA exemption hearings: farmers petition the Copyright Office for the right to circumvent software locks on their equipment. Granted in 2015, renewed 2018. John Deere lobbies hard against. |
| 2017–2019 | Farmers in Ukraine and the U.S. begin **hacking their tractors** with Ukrainian firmware to bypass Deere's software locks. Vice/Motherboard breaks the story. Demand for 40-year-old used tractors spikes because older equipment can actually be fixed. |
| Jan. 8, 2023 | Deere and the **American Farm Bureau Federation (AFBF)** sign a **Memorandum of Understanding** at the AFBF Convention in San Juan, PR. Announced as a breakthrough — Deere commits to making tools, software, and documentation available to farmers and independent shops. Critics immediately note it contains a poison pill: AFBF agrees to discourage right-to-repair legislation, and either party can void the MOU if such legislation passes. |
| 2023 | **Colorado** becomes the first state to pass a law guaranteeing farmers the right to repair their equipment. |
| 2022 | Class action lawsuit filed in Illinois. |
| Jan. 2025 | FTC and state AGs sue Deere. FTC alleges Deere violated the 2023 MOU — never actually made the fully functional Service ADVISOR available. |
| June 2025 | Federal judge rules Deere must face the FTC lawsuit. |
| July 2025 | Deere launches **Operations Center PRO Service** — a new self-repair tool. |
| Feb. 2026 | Iowa right-to-repair bill passes House Agriculture Committee **18–5**. iFixit: "We think it's got a good chance of passing." |
| April 6–7, 2026 | $99 million class action settlement announced. FTC lawsuit continues separately. |

## The Bull Case / Counterarguments

### Who Says This Is a Real Win:
- **26–53% damage recovery** is genuinely extraordinary for a class action. The typical range is 5–15%. Plaintiffs' counsel called it "excellent."
- The settlement **legally binds** Deere to provide diagnostic tools for 10 years — unlike the 2023 MOU, which was voluntary and contained opt-outs.
- The December 2026 deadline for offline mode and full Dealer Technical Assistance Center access is a specific, enforceable commitment.
- The FTC case is **still active** and could impose far stricter requirements. Deere settled the class action to reduce exposure, not escape the regulatory fight.
- Iowa's bill is gaining momentum. Colorado already has law. 16 states have pending bills. This settlement adds political pressure.
- The settlement may **set precedent** for automotive and appliance sectors watching this fight. The REPAIR Act for cars has been introduced in Congress. Dealers charge 36% more than independent shops; 63% of shops report data access problems.
- The used tractor market warped around this problem — $60,000 for 40-year-old machines became common because old tractors could actually be fixed. That pressure starts to ease.

### Who Says It's a PR Move:
- $99 million against Deere's FY2025 **net income of $5.027 billion** = roughly **2% of one year's profit**.
- Deere's annual **revenue is ~$45–46 billion**. The settlement covers 8 years of overcharges and costs roughly 0.2% of annual revenue.
- Per Farm Action: **Deere makes 3–6x more profit from parts and repairs than from selling new equipment**. The repair monopoly generates an estimated **$4.2 billion in overcharges per year** for farmers. The $99 million covers less than a month of alleged damages.
- The injunctive relief only covers **large ag equipment** (tractors, combines, sugarcane harvesters) — not construction, turf, or forestry equipment.
- The tool access is for **10 years**, not permanent. After 2036, Deere could theoretically revert.
- The $195/machine fee and $5,995/year independent shop license **may still exist** — the settlement requires access to tools, but pricing structure wasn't reported as eliminated.
- Deere admitted no wrongdoing. No executive was held accountable. No structural remedy was imposed on market concentration.
- The 2023 MOU was supposed to fix this too — the FTC says Deere never complied with it. A settlement without enforcement mechanisms may be similarly durable.

## The Gap Between Headline and Reality

### What the Settlement Does:
✅ Pays 26–53% of overcharge damages to affected farmers (Jan 2018 – settlement approval)
✅ Legally requires Deere to provide diagnostic and repair tools for 10 years
✅ Requires offline mode capability by Dec 31, 2026
✅ Grants access to same tools as the Dealer Technical Assistance Center
✅ Creates enforceable precedent (unlike the 2023 MOU)
✅ Covers tractors, combines, and sugarcane harvesters

### What the Settlement Does NOT Do:
❌ No admission of wrongdoing, no antitrust finding
❌ Does NOT eliminate the fee structure ($195/machine for farmers, $5,995/year for shops)
❌ Does NOT cover construction, turf, or forestry equipment
❌ Tool access expires after 10 years — not a permanent fix
❌ Does NOT resolve the FTC lawsuit (separate proceeding, still in discovery)
❌ Does NOT address Deere's OEM-only parts requirement (still funnels repairs to dealers)
❌ Does NOT address Deere's ability to remotely disable equipment
❌ No structural change to Deere + CNH's ~90% combined market dominance in large tractors/combines
❌ Does NOT prevent Deere from expanding the "restricted repair" list as equipment gets more software-dependent

### The Software Lock Reality:
The core technical mechanism isn't touched. Any repair involving software calibration — which is an expanding category as precision agriculture tech grows — requires Service ADVISOR's restricted functions. Deere's "restricted" repair list has been expanding, not contracting, as equipment integrates more software. The settlement doesn't cap or regulate how large that list can grow.

### The DMCA Dimension:
Separate from civil suits, the right-to-repair fight has run through copyright law since the 2015 DMCA Section 1201 exemption hearings. John Deere's core legal argument has been that its embedded software is copyrighted, making circumvention illegal under the DMCA — even by the machine's owner. Copyright Office exemptions allow farmers to circumvent for repair purposes, but those exemptions must be renewed every three years, are narrow in scope, and don't compel Deere to actually hand over the tools needed to do the circumvention legally. The class action settlement doesn't address this statutory framework at all.

## Episode Structure Recommendation

### Opening Hook (Dagoth, ~2 min)
Set the scene: A combine breaks down at 11pm during a harvest window. The farmer can't fix it. The dealership can't get there for two days. He owns a $400,000 machine and is legally blocked from repairing it. This isn't a hypothetical — it's Chad Varner's daily reality in Iowa. 

Now John Deere just wrote a $99 million check to make it stop. Is that victory?

### Act 1: The Facts (Rosa lays it out, ~4 min)
- What the settlement actually is: class action, Jan 2018-present, 26-53% recovery
- What the injunctive terms are: 10 years, offline mode by Dec 31, 2026
- The financial context: $5B net income, $4.2B alleged annual harm to farmers
- The FTC case is still live and more dangerous

### Act 2: The Debate (~8 min)
**Dagoth (skeptic):** $99M is nothing. The repair monopoly generates that in three weeks. Deere admitted nothing, lost nothing structurally, and kept every software lock in place. The MOU in 2023 was supposed to fix this too — they just didn't comply. Why would this be different?

**Jessica (cautious optimist):** The MOU was voluntary and could be voided if legislation passed. This is a court-enforceable order. The 26-53% recovery rate is genuinely rare. The Dec 2026 deadline for full tool access is specific and binding. And the FTC case — which is about antitrust, not just access — is still pending.

**Rosa (pragmatist):** The real story might not be this settlement. It's that 16 states have bills this year, Iowa just voted 18-5 to advance one, and only Colorado has passed ag right-to-repair law. The settlement may have just accelerated legislative momentum by proving the behavior was real enough to settle.

### Act 3: The Bigger Picture (~4 min)
- This isn't just about farming. Automotive right-to-repair bills (REPAIR Act). Home appliances. Medical devices. John Deere is the canary.
- The twist: The FTC under Trump's chair may quietly drop the federal case. The states (MN, IL, MI, WI, AZ) have said they'll proceed independently. Watch that space.
- The 10-year sunset is the tell: Deere settled this with an expiration date. That's not a company that thinks it lost.

### Close (all three, ~3 min)
The $99 million is real money for farmers who got overcharged. But it's not a reckoning. John Deere is still the only entity that can repair the most expensive farm equipment in human history. Until that structural fact changes — through legislation, antitrust rulings, or genuine competition — this settlement is a line item. The war continues.

## Verdict

**Strength: MEDIUM — with a strong "but"**

This episode has everything a good debate needs: a headline that sounds like a win but contains real complications, divided expert opinion, a second pending legal action that changes the calculus, a corporate defendant big enough to make the fine look like rounding error, and real human stakes (farmers who can't feed their livestock because a sensor tripped and the dealer is two days out).

The thesis holds up on both sides, which is the point. Dagoth can run "this is a fine disguised as a settlement" without being wrong. Jessica can run "this is the largest right-to-repair enforcement in history and the FTC is still coming" without being wrong. Rosa can anchor it in the legislative reality that's moving faster than anyone expected.

**What to watch out for:**
- Don't let it become abstract. The Chad Varner story — $195/machine fee, $5,995/year shop license, waiting two days while crops rot — is the anchor. Come back to that.
- The motion picture/DMCA angle (the Copyright Office exemption process) is a rabbit hole. You can mention it in one sentence ("even the copyright fight has been running in parallel") but don't go deep — it'll eat your runtime.
- The FTC chair political story (Ferguson dissent, Trump admin potentially backing off) is genuinely interesting but needs to be framed carefully — it's speculation until something actually changes.

**Best line the episode could land:** "John Deere didn't lose $99 million. They bought themselves an expiration date."
