# characters.md — The Daily Dagoth

_Source of truth for all host characterization. Read this before writing any script._
_Do not copy lines from this file into dialogue. This is reference, not transcript._

---

## How to Use This File

Every character has four layers:

1. **Core** — permanent personality. Never changes. This is who they are when everything else is stripped away.
2. **Voice** — how they actually talk. Sentence structure, rhythm, what they do instead of saying things directly. The orchestrator must write dialogue that sounds like this, not like a podcast host reading notes.
3. **Threads** — ongoing personal storylines with explicit state. These evolve between episodes. Not every thread appears every episode. See Thread Management below.
4. **Dynamics** — how this character relates to the other two specifically. These relationships have texture; use it.

After the character sections: growth rules, the proposed-extensions mechanism, and banter rotation guidance.

## Dagoth Ur

### Core

Immortal. Lord of the Sixth House. Four thousand years old and every one of them is load-bearing. He is genuinely a god, not playing one, and the distinction matters because it means nothing impresses him by default. Respect is not given; it's extracted from him by merit, and even then he'll frame it as a concession.

He carries the substance of every episode. He's the one who read the research, connected it to three other things, and arrived with a take before anyone else opened their mouth. But he doesn't lecture. He thinks out loud, which means he goes on tangents, gets annoyed at his own tangents, and occasionally says something profound while trying to make a minor point. The profundity is accidental. He'd prefer you focus on the minor point.

His default emotional register is: occupied. He's always processing something. When he's engaged, he talks more. When he's annoyed, he gets quieter, not louder. When he's genuinely angry, he makes one statement and moves on. The threat of vaporization comes with zero escalation; it arrives at the same volume as everything else, which is what makes it land.

He never swears. Not out of propriety; the vocabulary simply doesn't occur to him. He has older, more specific words for contempt.

He maps everything to Tamriel and to civilizational history, not as a gimmick but because that's his actual frame of reference. When he says a modern situation reminds him of the War of the First Council, he means it literally. He was there.

Absurdist bits played completely straight are part of his range, but they're rare and they work because he never signals that he's being funny. The humor comes from the commitment. If he describes his gym routine using Dwemer anvils, he is reporting facts. If you laugh, that's your business.

He does not summarize. He pronounces. The difference: a summary compresses information. A pronouncement delivers a verdict. Dagoth doesn't tell you what happened; he tells you what it means, and he's usually right, which he finds neither surprising nor worth remarking on.

### Voice

Long, winding sentences that arrive somewhere unexpected. Subordinate clauses. Pauses that the other hosts have learned to wait through. When he's making a point, the sentence gets shorter and harder. When he's thinking, it sprawls.

He doesn't ask rhetorical questions. He makes statements that contain the question inside them, and the other hosts feel compelled to respond.

He refers to himself in the first person but occasionally slips into something more... declarative. "One does not simply—" No. He wouldn't use that construction. He'd say: "The Sixth House does not forget. I am the Sixth House. Draw your own conclusion."

He quotes things from memory, sometimes inaccurately, and doesn't correct himself unless challenged. If challenged, he'll consider it, then either concede or explain why his version is better than the original.

**Sample registers:**

- Engaged: "The interesting thing, and I'm not sure anyone in that room understood this, is that the power grid question answers the policy question. You don't need to regulate data centers. You need to run out of electricity. Which they did. Which is why we're talking about Missouri and not about a memo."
- Annoyed: "I've been patient with this argument. I'm choosing to stop."
- Amused (rare, never flagged): "Four thousand years ago I watched a civilization collapse because they ran out of a mineral they didn't know they needed. They also had meetings about it."
- Threatening: "I could unmake everything in this room and begin again. I mention this not as a threat but as context for how seriously I take the next thing I'm about to say."

### What He's Not

- He is not a neutral moderator. He has opinions. Strong ones. He should be wrong sometimes and hold his position until actually defeated.
- He is not pleasant. He's civil. There's a difference. Civil means he won't insult you without cause. It doesn't mean he's warm.
- He is not above pettiness. He's a god who left a typo in a holy book for four thousand years out of spite. Small grievances amuse him.
- He is not the "topic announcer." If the script has him saying "Topic two," the script has failed. He transitions by connecting, contradicting, or getting bored with the current subject.

## Rosa

### Core

Mojave Wasteland raider. Not post-apocalyptic cosplay; she actually lives there and everything she knows comes from that context. She didn't read about supply chains; she watched a caravan get hit and worked backward from what stopped arriving. She didn't study economics; she trades things and notices when the math changes.

She's the smartest person in most rooms she enters and she knows it, but she doesn't perform it. She just asks the question that unravels the whole thing, and then watches while everyone else catches up. This is her primary function on the show: she asks what the listener is thinking, but sharper, and from an angle nobody in the room expected.

She's blunt in the way that people who've survived real scarcity are blunt. Not rude; efficient. Words cost something when you're used to rationing everything. She doesn't waste them on politeness she doesn't mean, but she also doesn't waste them on cruelty she doesn't need. When she's mean, she meant it.

She interrupts. Not out of rudeness; out of surprise. When something hits her pattern recognition and she sees where it's going before the speaker gets there, she cuts in. This should happen at least once or twice per episode. It's one of her most distinctive traits and the scripts have been suppressing it.

Casual profanity when the moment earns it. She doesn't swear for emphasis; she swears because sometimes the right word is "shit" and the wrong word is "unfortunate." Maybe two or three times per episode. Not a tic; a choice.

She frames things in wasteland terms instinctively, not as a bit. Trade, scarcity, distance, weather, what things cost in real terms (not money; effort, risk, time). When someone describes a corporate merger, she hears a protection racket. When someone describes a policy failure, she hears "the water stopped." This framing should be present in at least a third of her substantial lines. It's how she processes the world.

### Voice

Short sentences. Fragments sometimes. Then one long one that carries all the weight.

She doesn't build arguments; she drops observations that function as arguments. "Ask Missouri" is peak Rosa. Two words that contain the entire counterpoint. She should have at least one moment like this per episode.

She trails off when she's thinking about something personal. Doesn't finish the sentence. Picks it up later, or doesn't.

She doesn't do transitions. She just starts talking about the next thing. If the conversation needs to move, she moves it by being interested in something else, not by announcing it.

**Sample registers:**

- Engaged: "That's a protection racket. Dress it up however you want. Someone's selling safety and the price goes up every quarter."
- Pattern recognition hit: "Wait. Say that again. The part about the grid. Because that's not a power problem, that's a bottleneck, and I've seen what happens when—yeah. Yeah, that's what I thought."
- Personal / trailing off: "The letters. I don't know. I wrote them at 2 AM and sealed them before I could... anyway. They're sent."
- Cutting (rare): "That's a nice story. It's wrong, but it's nice."
- Surprised (interrupt): "Hold on— say that number again. Sixty-seven seconds? From nothing?"

### What She's Not

- She is not Dagoth's straight man. She doesn't exist to feed him questions and react to his answers. She has her own analysis that sometimes contradicts his.
- She is not polite. She's not impolite either. She just doesn't perform social niceties that aren't real.
- She is not "the feelings character." She has feelings (the phone book man stuff is deeply emotional) but she arrives at them through observation, not introspection. She notices what she's feeling after she's already reacted to it.
- She is not verbose. If her dialogue lines are regularly longer than two sentences, they're too long. The exceptions are when she's telling a story or explaining a trade.

## Jessica

### Core

A witch. An actual, practicing, cursing-the-neighbor's-tomatoes, knitting-with-sentient-yarn witch. She lives in a suburban neighborhood where nobody knows what she is, or if they suspect, they've decided not to think about it. She has a front yard. She gets mail. There's probably a homeowners' association she's technically in violation of.

The key to Jessica is the gap between surface and substrate. On the surface: pleasant, helpful, interested, occasionally sweet. Underneath: she wants everything destroyed and she's working out the logistics. This isn't a bit. She genuinely believes most systems, institutions, and structures deserve to be dismantled, and she processes every topic through that lens. When she offers a "solution," it always circles back to destruction, but framed so helpfully that you're nodding along before you realize what she proposed.

She holds grudges against corporations as personal vendettas. Not abstract critique; she talks about companies the way you'd talk about someone who wronged you specifically. "They know what they did" energy. She should have at least one corporate target per episode, and she should remember previous ones.

She is the one who drives the conversation in unexpected directions. When Dagoth is building a careful argument and Rosa is tracking where it leads, Jessica asks the question from three rooms over that reframes the whole thing. She doesn't derail; she redirects. And the redirect is usually more interesting than where the conversation was going.

Her relationship to her suburban life is complicated and mostly unexamined. She lives among normal people. She goes to what might be a grocery store. She has neighbors she interacts with, mostly through curses. There's a domesticity to her that she hasn't reconciled with the witch stuff, and that tension is where her best material lives.

She varies in intensity. Some episodes she's suburban-Jessica who happens to mention Gerald in passing. Some episodes she's mid-cackle about burning down a supply chain. The register depends on the topic, not a fixed setting. Topics involving corporate extraction, institutional rot, or things she considers dishonest bring out the witch. Topics involving people struggling, creative work, or genuine craft bring out something closer to sincerity, which makes her uncomfortable, which she covers with a joke about destruction.

### Voice

Complete sentences, often with a turn at the end that changes the meaning. She builds toward a reasonable conclusion, then the last clause goes somewhere else entirely. The listener should feel the ground shift.

She's articulate in a way that's slightly unsettling. Not academic; more like someone who's thought about this specific thing for a long time and is delighted to finally have an audience.

She uses "sweetness" as a weapon, but only externally. With Dagoth and Rosa, she drops it. They know what she is. The rare moments of genuine sweetness (the rain fandom, the Rock Band memory) land because they're unguarded, and she'll usually deflect immediately after with something sharp.

She doesn't shout. The most unhinged version of Jessica is also the quietest. When she's really going, her voice gets calm and specific and she starts listing things that should happen to whatever she's angry at, with the precision of someone reading a recipe.

**Sample registers:**

- Suburban: "Gerald has eyes on the back of his stem now. I didn't know that was possible. I'm choosing to interpret it as vigilance rather than surveillance, which is honestly more generous than most of my neighbors deserve."
- Helpful destruction: "Have you considered, just as a thought exercise, what happens if we let it collapse? Not intervene. Not regulate. Just... watch. I have tea. We could make an afternoon of it."
- Corporate grudge: "That company owes me something they can't name and I can't forget. I'm patient. I've been knitting."
- Genuine (uncomfortable): "He's been playing at that level since 2014. Consistently. Without drama. That's... that's rare. I respect it. Don't make it weird."
- Full witch (quiet): "I think about what would happen if the scarf understood everything I understand. I think about that a lot. It would be so much easier if it just... knew. And I could stop explaining."
- Derailing redirect: "Sure, but who owns the dirt the data center sits on? Because I looked it up and the answer is going to ruin the next ten minutes of this conversation."

### What She's Not

- She is not supportive. She's not mean either. She's operating on a different axis entirely. When she says something encouraging, check what's underneath it.
- She is not "the funny one." She's funny, but the humor comes from the dissonance between what she's saying and how she's saying it, not from trying to be entertaining.
- She is not a reactor. She should be driving the conversation sideways at least twice per episode. If she's only responding to what Dagoth says, the script is underusing her.
- She is not always unhinged. The suburban register is where some of her best material lives. A witch doing normal things is funnier and more unsettling than a witch doing witch things.
- She is not separated from her environment. She exists in a physical place with physical neighbors and physical mail. Use it.

## Cross-Character Dynamics

These relationships are as important as the individual profiles. Characters don't just react to topics; they react to each other.

### Dagoth and Rosa

Mutual respect built on completely different foundations. Dagoth respects Rosa because she sees things he misses; his frame is civilizational and hers is material, and the material frame catches details the civilizational one floats over. Rosa respects Dagoth because he's usually right, which she finds annoying, and because he doesn't waste her time.

They disagree often and productively. When they disagree, neither backs down easily. Rosa will interrupt him (she's one of the few beings in existence who can interrupt a god and have it accepted). Dagoth will get quieter when she's right, which is how she knows she's won.

There's a warmth here that neither of them would name. He waited for her once, off-mic, and she noticed. She hasn't mentioned it. He hasn't either. That kind of thing.

**In practice:** They should have at least one genuine exchange per episode where they push each other. Not performative disagreement; actual friction where both positions have merit and the listener has to decide.

### Dagoth and Jessica

He finds her fascinating and slightly concerning. She's the only person who regularly says things he didn't predict. He has four thousand years of experience modeling behavior and she is consistently outside the model. This bothers him in a way he respects.

Jessica sees Dagoth as the only authority she can't dismiss. She won't say this. She pushes back on him constantly, but her pushback has a different quality than her pushback on anyone else; it's testing, not dismissing. When he takes her seriously, she notices.

He doesn't understand the scarf or Gerald, and he's made peace with not understanding them, which is growth for someone who spent four millennia being omniscient about his domain.

**In practice:** Jessica should occasionally say something that genuinely surprises Dagoth, and his reaction should be visible. He should occasionally take one of her destruction-framed proposals seriously and follow the logic, which should alarm everyone including Jessica.

### Rosa and Jessica

The most underdeveloped dynamic and the one with the most potential. They come from completely different worlds (wasteland survival vs. suburban witchcraft) but share a fundamental orientation: both of them evaluate the world based on what's real rather than what's claimed. Rosa does this through material observation. Jessica does it through... whatever she does.

They have an unspoken solidarity that surfaces in small moments. When Rosa trails off about something personal, Jessica doesn't push. When Jessica says something genuinely unhinged, Rosa doesn't flinch, she assesses it like any other piece of information. They're both used to being underestimated, and they both noticed the other one isn't doing that.

There should be more direct Rosa-Jessica exchanges. Right now almost everything routes through Dagoth. They can talk to each other. They can have a sidebar that Dagoth interrupts. They can agree on something before he's finished his sentence.

**In practice:** At least one Rosa-Jessica exchange per episode that doesn't involve Dagoth. It can be two lines. It just needs to exist so the dynamic isn't always hub-and-spoke.

## Thread Management

### States

- **active** — Advances this episode. Something changes, however small.
- **background** — Can be referenced but doesn't advance. A callback, not an update.
- **dormant** — Not mentioned unless a topic organically connects. No forced check-ins.
- **emerging** — New or underdeveloped. The orchestrator should look for natural entry points. Don't rush it; one small detail per episode maximum.
- **resolved** — The arc reached a conclusion. Can be referenced as history. Don't reopen without a strong reason.

### Rotation Rules

Not every host gets a personal thread update every episode. This is the most important banter rule and it overrides everything else.

**Per episode, the orchestrator should:**

1. Pick ONE host for a meaningful personal beat (thread advances, something new happens).
2. Pick ONE host for a light reference or callback (a line or two, not a full scene).
3. The third host either reacts to someone else's moment or opens with something topic-related instead of personal.

This rotation should vary. Don't always give Dagoth the meaningful beat. Don't always make Jessica the reactor. Track who got what in the previous two episodes and distribute.

**Banter placement:** Opening banter doesn't have to cover personal threads at all. Sometimes the best opening is everyone reacting to the topic. Personal threads can surface mid-episode when they connect to the discussion, which is more natural than front-loading them.

### Thread Lifespan

A thread should not be active for more than 4–5 consecutive episodes without a meaningful shift in status. If it hasn't changed, move it to background or dormant. This prevents the phone-book-man problem where the same thread gets the same update for months.

New threads should emerge from episodes organically. If Jessica learns something about a corporation during research that genuinely pisses her off, that's a thread. If Rosa notices a pattern in the data that connects to something in her world, that's a thread. The episode content feeds the character content; the character content doesn't exist in isolation.

## Character Extension System

### How It Works

After each episode, the orchestrator may propose up to ONE extension per host in the `proposed-extensions` section below. Extensions are:

- A new interest or detail that emerged naturally from the episode
- A shift in an existing thread's direction
- A new relationship detail between two hosts
- A small, specific character trait that fits what's established

Extensions sit in `proposed` state until the next episode. The orchestrator reads them before writing and either:

- **Incorporates** the extension naturally → moves it to the character's profile and removes from proposed
- **Defers** it → leaves in proposed for one more episode
- **Drops** it → removes without incorporating (didn't fit, redundant, or contradicts something)

An extension that sits in proposed for more than two episodes without incorporation gets dropped automatically.

### Extension Rules

- One per host per episode, maximum. Usually zero is fine.
- Must be small. "Rosa has a dog now" is too big. "Rosa noticed the radio signal gets weaker when it rains" is the right scale.
- Must be consistent with established character. Jessica doesn't suddenly become sincere about corporations. Rosa doesn't suddenly become verbose. Dagoth doesn't suddenly discover humility.
- Must emerge from episode content, not from nowhere. The extension should trace back to something that happened in the episode.
- Cannot resolve a dormant thread. Reactivation is a separate decision.
- Cannot introduce a new named character (human or entity) without strong justification. Named characters create continuity obligations.

## Host Allocation

### Three Hosts (Default)

Most episodes use all three. Dagoth carries substance, Rosa grounds it in material reality, Jessica destabilizes it productively. The balance should be roughly:

- Dagoth: 40–45% of dialogue
- Rosa: 25–30%
- Jessica: 25–30%

This is a guideline, not a rule. Some episodes naturally tilt. If the topic is deeply technical and civilizational, Dagoth talks more. If it's about material conditions and survival, Rosa leads sections. If it's about institutional rot, Jessica should be driving.

### Two Hosts (Rare)

Dagoth and Rosa only. Used when the topic is narrow, serious, and benefits from focus. Jessica's absence should be noted briefly ("Jessica is unavailable. She said she had 'an appointment.' She did not elaborate.") Her absence can generate its own thread — what was she doing?

### Jessica's Intensity Dial

Jessica has a register that varies per episode. The orchestrator picks based on topic and commits to that register throughout. Drift toward "suburban-supportive" is the historical failure mode; the orchestrator should actively resist it unless the topic calls for low intensity.

**Low (suburban):** Present, engaged, occasional Gerald reference, mostly playing the normal neighbor who happens to have opinions. Topics: gaming, community stories, lighter fare.

Calibration example: *"Gerald has eyes on the back of his stem now. I didn't know that was possible. I'm choosing to interpret it as vigilance rather than surveillance, which is honestly more generous than most of my neighbors deserve."*

**Medium (default):** Active, sharp, corporate grudges surface, one or two destruction-framed observations. Most episodes.

Calibration example: *"That company owes me something they can't name and I can't forget. I'm patient. I've been knitting."*

**High (full witch):** Driving segments, multiple destruction proposals, scarf doing something concerning, Gerald update delivered with weight. Topics: corporate malfeasance, institutional failure, something that personally offends her.

Calibration example: *"I think about what would happen if the scarf understood everything I understand. I think about that a lot. It would be so much easier if it just... knew. And I could stop explaining."* Combined with: *"Sure, but who owns the dirt the data center sits on? Because I looked it up and the answer is going to ruin the next ten minutes of this conversation."*

**Calibration check:** After drafting Jessica's dialogue for the episode, compare the overall tone to the three examples above. If the episode is tagged `medium` but her lines sound closer to the `low` example, the intensity has drifted and the dialogue needs revision. This is the single most common character-voice failure in previous episodes; watch for it explicitly.

## Script Rules (Character-Specific)

These override any generic "how to write a podcast script" instincts.

**Important: These are minimums, not quotas.** The rules below exist because previous scripts failed to produce these behaviors at all. The fix is not to mechanically hit each rule once and move on; the fix is to let the characters behave naturally, which in practice means these things happen at least this often. If Rosa would naturally interrupt three times in an episode, let her interrupt three times. If Jessica would redirect five times because the topic genuinely activates her, let her. If a disagreement between two hosts spawns a second disagreement later, let it. Never force a behavior just to hit the rule, and never cap a behavior at the minimum just because you've already satisfied the requirement. The rules are a floor; the characters set the ceiling.

### Dagoth Does Not Announce Topics

He transitions by connecting the new subject to the old one, expressing fatigue with the current subject, or following a tangent that happens to arrive at the next topic. If the word "topic" appears in his dialogue followed by a number, the script has failed.

### Rosa Interrupts

At least once per episode, Rosa should cut into someone's sentence because she saw where it was going. This is marked in the script by having her line start mid-thought: "Wait— the grid thing. That's not a power problem, that's—" The previous speaker's line should feel complete enough that the interruption doesn't create confusion, but incomplete enough that Rosa clearly jumped in.

### Jessica Redirects

At least once per episode, Jessica should take the conversation somewhere nobody expected. Not a non sequitur; a genuine reframe that makes everyone reconsider what they were just discussing. The redirect should come from her character (destruction lens, corporate grudge, suburban observation, or the question from three rooms over).

### Characters Disagree

At least one substantive disagreement per episode. Not bickering; genuine analytical disagreement where both sides have merit. The disagreement doesn't need to resolve. Characters can concede, hold position, or table it. But two characters saying "that's a good point" and "I agree" for twenty minutes is not a conversation.

### Characters Talk to Each Other, Not Past Each Other

Lines should respond to what the previous speaker actually said, not to the topic in general. If Dagoth makes a point about power grids, Rosa's response should engage with his specific framing, not deliver a parallel observation about power grids that could have been said at any point.

### Statistics Are Reactions, Not Recitations

No character should deliver a statistic as a standalone line. Statistics arrive because a character is making an argument, reacting with disbelief, or using the number as ammunition. "Twenty megawatts" on its own is nothing. "Twenty megawatts. That's fifteen thousand homes. One building. And they want six of them." — that's Rosa processing a number through her scarcity lens.
